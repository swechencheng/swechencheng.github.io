    Are we slowly building the world of Psycho-Pass?

    1 minute read

    Are we slowly building the world of Psycho-Pass?

    I came across a recent news about a woman in Florida who reportedly used Claude as a kind of diary. After she allegedly wrote about attacking a sheriff’s office, the conversation was flagged by Claude’s safety systems, escalated to human review, and ultimately reported to law enforcement.

    What struck me was not simply the question of whether the report was justified.

    It was the resemblance to an idea from Psycho-Pass:

    “Psycho-Pass is set in a futuristic Japan governed by the Sibyl System (シビュラシステム, Shibyura Shisutemu), a powerful biomechatronic computer network which continually monitors the psychological traits of Japanese citizens using a ‘cymatic scan.’”

    — Psycho-Pass, via Wikipedia

    When Psycho-Pass first appeared in 2012, this was science fiction.

    Today, we are building systems that can continuously interact with us through natural language, observe what we choose to tell them, identify patterns in our thoughts and behavior, assess potential risks, and—under certain circumstances—escalate those findings to humans or authorities.

    Of course, this is not (yet?) the Sibyl System.

    AI cannot simply scan someone’s mind and calculate a “Crime Coefficient.”

    But there is an important conceptual shift happening:

    The interface through which we express our thoughts is becoming an intelligent observer.

    And unlike a traditional search engine or social network, an AI assistant is designed to encourage us to reveal things we might never post publicly. People use it as a therapist, a diary, a confidant, a brainstorming partner, and sometimes even as a substitute for human conversation.

    That makes the privacy question fundamentally different.

    The interesting question may no longer be:

    “Can AI read my mind?”

    but rather:

    “What happens when people voluntarily tell AI what is in their mind?”

    Psycho-Pass imagined a future in which society could measure the human psyche at scale.

    Generative AI may be giving us the first practical infrastructure to make something resembling that idea possible—not through a single omniscient system, but through billions of intimate human-machine conversations.

    And that deserves much more discussion than simply asking whether AI is “safe” or “unsafe.”
